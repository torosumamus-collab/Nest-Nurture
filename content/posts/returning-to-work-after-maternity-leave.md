The night before returning to work can bring a mix of emotions. You may be excited to reconnect with colleagues, grateful for your career, and at the same time heartbroken about leaving your baby for the first time. These feelings can exist together, and they don't make you any less committed to your family or your work.

Returning to work after maternity leave isn't simply about changing your routine. It's a transition for your entire family, and like any major life change, it deserves patience, preparation, and kindness toward yourself.

## Why Returning to Work Feels So Emotional

Going back to work after maternity leave rarely feels like flipping a switch back to "normal," because nothing about this season is quite the same as before. You're not just resuming a job. You're stepping back into an identity that now has to make room for a completely new one, and that adjustment touches more than your calendar.

Part of what makes it hard is how many feelings show up at once. There's guilt about leaving your baby, even when you know they're in good hands. There's genuine excitement about using your brain in a different way, seeing colleagues, or simply having a cup of coffee that's still hot by the time you drink it. Anxiety tends to arrive too, about missed milestones, about whether your baby will be okay, about whether you'll be okay. And underneath all of it, for many families, there's a financial reality that makes returning to work necessary rather than optional, which can add its own quiet weight.

None of these feelings cancel each other out. You can grieve the loss of your old, slower routine and look forward to your first team meeting in the same week, sometimes the same morning. Mixed emotions here aren't a red flag. They're simply what this transition looks like for most mothers.

> You can love your baby deeply and still enjoy your career.

## Preparing Before Your First Day Back

A little preparation in the days before you return can take some of the edge off that first morning. It won't make the transition painless, but it can make it feel more manageable.

Consider, in the week or two beforehand:

1. **Visiting childcare ahead of time**, so the space, the caregivers, and the routine feel familiar to both you and your baby before you actually need to walk away.
2. **Practicing your new morning routine** a few times, start to finish, so you know roughly how long it takes and where the friction points are.
3. **Prepping clothes and simple meals** in advance, even just for the first few days, so decision fatigue doesn't pile up on top of everything else.
4. **Organizing pumping supplies**, if you're breastfeeding, including a plan for where and when you'll pump during your workday.
5. **Test-driving your commute**, especially if drop-off is now part of it, to see how the timing actually plays out.
6. **Talking with your employer** about your schedule, any accommodations you need, and what a gradual return might look like if that's an option.
7. **Setting realistic expectations** for yourself, particularly for that first week. It's allowed to be imperfect.

**Before You Go Back, Have Ready:**

✔ Childcare arrangements confirmed
✔ A few days of meals planned
✔ Outfits chosen, yours and your baby's
✔ Your work bag packed
✔ Your baby's daycare bag packed
✔ Your commute tested at least once
✔ A conversation with your manager about expectations
✔ Realistic expectations set for week one
✔ At least one piece of quality family time scheduled
✔ A reminder to be kind to yourself
## Easing Into a New Routine

It's tempting to want your new routine to work perfectly from day one. It won't, and that's fine. What actually helps is building in enough slack that a rough morning doesn't unravel the whole day.

A slightly earlier bedtime for everyone, simpler dinners that don't require much thought, and a shared family calendar can all take pressure off in small but meaningful ways. So can genuinely sharing responsibilities rather than quietly carrying most of them yourself, and asking for help before you're at your limit rather than after. Try to hold your routine loosely at first. What works in week one may need adjusting by week three, and that's not failure, it's just information.

> **Nest & Nurture Tip:** Your routine should support your family, not exhaust it.

## Managing the Emotional Side of Returning

Alongside the logistics, there's an emotional undercurrent that deserves just as much attention.

Mom guilt tends to show up in specific, sometimes surprising moments: tears after drop-off, a pang of worry during a meeting, a strange mix of relief and sadness on the drive to work. Missing your baby during the day is common, even when you know they're safe and cared for. Some mothers also describe feeling disconnected at first, from their baby, from their pre-baby self, or from both, before things start to settle.

At the same time, many mothers describe something unexpected: the relief of feeling a bit more like themselves again, having a role outside of caregiving, even while still deeply missing their baby. Both experiences are normal, and neither one cancels out the other. Every emotion that shows up in this transition, even the contradictory ones, deserves to be treated as valid rather than something to push past quickly.

## Caring for Yourself During the Transition

It's easy to let your own needs slide to the bottom of the list during a transition like this, but a little self-care goes a long way toward making the whole season more sustainable.

That might look like drinking your morning coffee without rushing it, even for five extra minutes. A short walk during lunch. A healthy meal instead of whatever's fastest. Staying hydrated through a busier day than you're used to. Protecting sleep wherever you realistically can. Calling a friend just to say the day was hard. Starting therapy, or returning to it, if you feel like you need the support. Journaling for a few minutes before bed, even just a line or two.

None of these need to be elaborate to help. The goal isn't a perfect self-care routine. It's a handful of small anchors that remind you you're a person too, not just a mother and an employee running on empty.

## Staying Connected with Your Baby

One of the quiet fears many mothers carry is that going back to work will somehow weaken the bond with their baby. It won't, but it does mean being intentional about connection in the hours you do have.

A few ways families find this rhythm:

- **A special bedtime routine** that's just yours, the same few steps each night.
- **Morning cuddles** before the rush of getting ready begins.
- **No-phone evenings**, even just for an hour, so your attention is fully with your baby.
- **Weekend traditions**, like a Saturday morning walk or a regular breakfast ritual.
- **Babywearing after work**, which offers closeness without needing to "do" anything.
- **Reading together**, even a single short book.
- **Bath time**, which often becomes a natural, unhurried connection point.

Connection matters more than the total number of hours in a day. A focused twenty minutes of your full attention often means more to your baby, and to you, than a distracted afternoon together.

## Communicating with Your Employer

An honest, early conversation with your employer can make a real difference in how sustainable this season feels.

It's worth discussing flexible scheduling or remote work if either is available to you, and being clear about the boundaries you need, like protected lunch breaks or a firm end time most days. If you're pumping, ask about accommodations well before your first day so there's no scrambling to figure it out on the spot. It also helps to be upfront about your priorities right now and, if it's an option, to explore a gradual return rather than jumping straight back to a full schedule. Most workplaces respond better to a clear, calm conversation than to guessing what you need.

## When the Transition Feels Harder Than Expected

For most families, this season eases with time. For some, it doesn't, and that's worth paying attention to rather than pushing through quietly.

Persistent anxiety that doesn't lift, symptoms of depression, burnout that feels bottomless rather than temporary, or postpartum mental health struggles that are getting in the way of daily life are all reasons to reach out for professional support. This isn't a sign of weakness or of loving your family any less. It's simply recognizing that some transitions need more support than willpower alone can provide. If any of this sounds familiar, talk to your healthcare provider. They can help you figure out what kind of support makes sense for you.

## Remember That This Season Won't Last Forever

The first week back is not a preview of the rest of your career, and it's not a preview of the rest of your relationship with your baby either. It's just the first week.

Your baby will adjust. You will adjust. The mornings that feel chaotic now will, in time, feel routine. Give yourself the same patience you'd offer a friend going through this exact same thing.

## Gentle Reminder

You don't have to prove that you've "bounced back."

You don't have to be perfect at home and exceptional at work every single day.

This season is about finding a rhythm that works for your family, not anyone else's.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal to cry on the first day back at work?

Yes. Many mothers experience a mix of sadness, relief, excitement, and anxiety. Those feelings often ease as everyone settles into a new routine.

### Will my baby forget me?

No. Babies build secure attachments through consistent love and care over time. Returning to work doesn't change that bond.

### How long does it take to adjust?

Every family is different. Some mothers feel more settled within a few weeks, while others need a few months. Try not to compare your timeline with someone else's.

### Should I feel guilty for enjoying work?

Not at all. Loving your career and loving your child aren't competing feelings. Both can exist together.

## Conclusion

Returning to work after maternity leave isn't about leaving motherhood behind. It's about discovering how your career, your family, and your identity can grow together in this new season of life.

Give yourself permission to take it one day at a time. There will be mornings that feel rushed, afternoons that bring unexpected joy, and evenings that remind you why you're doing it all. Trust that, little by little, you'll find a rhythm that feels right for you and your family.
